Fake RoboSense LiDAR over TOLD
=============================

Two sensors, one pipeline, because that is how this looks with real hardware:

    fake_rs16.zig       pretends to be an RS-LiDAR-16      750 packets/s
    fake_helios32.zig   pretends to be an RS-Helios-32    1500 packets/s
        |  UDP :6699 (MSOP) and :7788 (DIFOP, 1/s) -- no qnnt in either fake
    rs16.zig            driver: first-generation MSOP in, model-independent scans out
    helios32.zig        driver: Helios MSOP in, the same scans out
    scan.zig            the vocabulary both produce, and the geometry maths
        |  (Zig modules, imported -- not processes)
    sensor.zig          the bridge: `lidar-sensor rs16` or `lidar-sensor helios32`
        |  topics "lidar/<model>" and "lidar/<model>/calibration"
    viewer.cpp          Qt/QML: one binary, either sensor -- it learns the geometry
    + main.qml

what the second driver settled
------------------------------

Adding the Helios-32 was the test of whether the first driver's boundaries were in the
right place. They mostly were, and the two places they were not are now fixed:

  * scan.zig did not exist. The first driver declared its own Sample, Block, Scan and
    Calibration, so a second driver would have produced structurally identical but
    unrelated types. Both now produce scan.zig's, so a consumer written against one works
    with the other.
  * the geometry was compiled into the viewer. Sixteen angles in a constant array, which is
    fine until a 32-channel sensor arrives. The angle table now travels on its own topic
    and the viewer reads channel count and firings-per-block out of each frame.

how the two models differ, which is the interesting part
--------------------------------------------------------

                    RS-LiDAR-16           RS-Helios-32
    channels        16                    32
    a block is      two firings of 16     one firing of 32
    blocks/packet   12                    12          (same 1248-byte packet)
    azimuths/rev    1800 in 900 blocks    1800 in 1800 blocks
    packets/s       750                   1500        at 600 rpm
    points/s        288,000               576,000
    distance unit   5 mm                  2.5 mm      same counts, half the range
    header          42 bytes, date        32 bytes, UTC seconds + microseconds
    angles          -15..+15, uniform     non-uniform, denser near the horizon

Both are 1248 bytes with 0xFFEE block flags and big-endian azimuths and distances, so only
the MSOP magic distinguishes them on the wire. Each driver checks it and refuses the
other's packets, and a test asserts both directions -- because a packet parsed by the wrong
driver would not error, it would produce plausible nonsense at half or double the range.

Replacing the fake with a real RS-LiDAR-16 means pointing the sensor at this host and
running nothing differently: the driver binds the same port either way. That is the reason
for the split -- the earlier version of this example generated the scene and packed TOLD in
one process, which demonstrated the transport but not how a driver is actually written.

what is being imitated
----------------------

RS-LiDAR-16 output, from its MSOP (Main data Stream Output Protocol) shape:

    1248-byte UDP packets   42-byte header, 12 blocks x 100 bytes, 6-byte tail
    12 blocks per packet    each block = one azimuth, two firings of 16 channels
    0.2 deg azimuth step    1800 azimuth positions per revolution
    600 RPM                 10 revolutions per second
    -> 900 blocks/rev, 75 packets/rev, 750 packets/s, 28,800 points/rev
    -> 288,000 points/s, about 0.94 MB/s on the wire

The sensor here publishes at that rate: one TOLD frame per MSOP packet, 750 frames/s, paced
by the library's scheduler at a 1333 us period.

Deliberate simplifications, since this is a fake and says so: one return per beam (no dual
return), no per-packet GPS timestamp block, and the two firings in a block share their
channel order rather than following the real interleave. Ranges come from a synthetic room
rather than from anything physical.

the frame, and why it is shaped this way
----------------------------------------

    Block   azimuth_cdeg  u16     hundredths of a degree, 0..35999, as MSOP carries it
            samples       bytes   32 x (u32 distance_mm, u8 intensity) = 160 bytes

    Packet  seq           u64     packet counter
            t_us          u64     the sensor's clock when the packet was assembled
            model         string  "RS-LiDAR-16"
            rpm           u16     600
            blocks        [12]Block

The judgment worth noticing is where the structure stops. Mapping every channel to a TOLD
object -- 384 objects per packet, each with named fields -- would put roughly ten times more
key bytes on the wire than sample bytes. Instead the block keeps its azimuth as a named
field, because a consumer wants it without parsing anything, and the 32 samples travel as
one BUFFER in exactly MSOP's layout.

That is TOLD used where self-description pays and got out of the way where it does not. The
sensor prints the comparison at startup; measured:

    raw MSOP              1248 bytes    no self-description at all
    per-block buffers     1552 bytes    what this uses: +304 bytes, or 24%
    per-channel objects   ~12 KB        every channel named, ten times the payload

304 bytes buys a frame that names its model, its rpm, its sequence, its timestamp and every
azimuth -- where the MSOP packet needs a datasheet to interpret at all.

geometry the viewer needs
-------------------------

The 16 channels are fixed vertical angles, -15 deg to +15 deg in 2 deg steps:

    channel 0..15  ->  -15, -13, -11, -9, -7, -5, -3, -1, +1, +3, +5, +7, +9, +11, +13, +15

A block's 32 samples are firing 0 (channels 0..15) at `azimuth_cdeg`, then firing 1
(channels 0..15) at `azimuth_cdeg + 20`, which is the 0.2 deg step. A point is then:

    r     = distance_mm / 1000
    block = azimuth_cdeg / 100 + firing * azimuth_step_cdeg / 100
    azim  = block + azimuth_offsets_deg[channel]

    x = r * cos(elev) * sin(azim) + lens_offset_m * sin(block)
    y = r * cos(elev) * cos(azim) + lens_offset_m * cos(block)
    z = r * sin(elev)

Two terms there were absent for a long time and both came from rs_driver's own decoder.
`azimuth_offsets_deg` is the per-channel horizontal correction, up to 5 degrees on a
two-column sensor -- see below, it is what split every wall in two. `lens_offset_m` is the
lens centre: the optics do not sit on the rotation axis, so a return leaves from a point a
few centimetres out along the block's pointing direction. Note which angle each uses -- the
beam carries the per-channel correction, the lens offset does not, because the offset is
where the mirror is aimed and the correction describes where each beam goes from there.

distance_mm of 0 means no return -- absorbed, sky, or outside the model's rated window --
and must be skipped
rather than plotted at the origin.

the scene
---------

A 20 m x 14 m room with the sensor at 1 m above the floor, two pillars, and one box that
moves, so a viewer shows something recognisable rather than noise: four walls, two circles,
and a rectangle that drifts across the floor. Intensity distinguishes wall from pillar from
floor, so a renderer colouring by intensity separates them.

the driver's honest boundaries
-----------------------------

What is faithful: the 1248-byte packet as 42-byte header, 12 blocks of 100 bytes, 6-byte
tail; the 8-byte MSOP magic; the 0xFFEE block flag; big-endian azimuths in hundredths of a
degree and big-endian distances -- network order, which is the single easiest thing to get
wrong in this format.

What is NOT verified against a datasheet, and is marked as such in rs16.zig: the offsets of
the fields inside the 42-byte header beyond the magic and the timestamp, and the DIFOP
layout (the fake's own, not RoboSense's). The distance unit IS now settled -- see below.

The Helios driver is now verified against a real RS-Helios-32, and most of what had been
guessed was wrong. What a live unit at 192.168.3.221 actually sends:

    field                  guessed          measured
    MSOP magic             8 bytes          4 bytes, 55 AA 05 5A
    MSOP header            32 bytes         42 bytes (first 0xFFEE observed at 42)
    MSOP tail              16 bytes         6 bytes
    timestamp offset       8                20, then 6 bytes of seconds + 4 of micros
    DIFOP length           1206             1248
    DIFOP rpm offset       8                8            -- one of two guesses that held
    DIFOP magic            A5FF005A11115555 the same     -- the other
    angle table            offset 32, 2B    offset 468, THREE bytes: sign + magnitude
    angle values           invented         +14.94 down to -54.60, non-uniform
    horizontal table       not known to     offset 564, same encoding: rs_driver's DIFOP
                           exist            is vert_angle_cali[32] then horiz_angle_cali[32]

The DIFOP also carries the ethernet block, which is how the layout was established at all:
sensor IP at 10, destination IP at 14, MAC at 18, MSOP port at 24, DIFOP port at 28. Reading
back 192.168.3.221, 192.168.3.100, 6699 and 7788 from a packet confirms the offsets are
right rather than coincidentally plausible.

The angle table is still a fallback rather than a default to rely on: it is ONE unit's
calibration, and the family covers several distributions. `anglesArePlaceholder()` reports
when it is in use, and the driver prefers the device's own table -- which arrives within a
second, as `angles device` in the bridge's output.

the lens centre offset
----------------------

    RS-LiDAR-16   lens_offset_m = 0.03825
    RS-Helios-32  lens_offset_m = 0.03498

rs_driver's mech const params call this RX, and it moves every point a constant 34.98 mm
(Helios) radially outward along the block azimuth -- measured over 15,357 real points, mean
and maximum both exactly that, which is what a constant radial term should look like.

It is applied at the BLOCK azimuth, not the per-channel corrected one, matching rs_driver:
`x = ... + RX * cos(angle_horiz)` there uses `angle_horiz` while the beam term uses
`angle_horiz_final`. Applying it at the corrected angle instead is wrong by
RX * sin(correction), about 3 mm for a Helios -- too small for any test that checked only
magnitudes, which is why there is one that checks the direction.

rs_driver also carries an RY of about a centimetre per model, a tangential offset, and never
uses it in its point computation. It is not applied here either. Matching the vendor's
driver is worth more than inventing a correction it declines to make -- but it is the one
remaining term in their own const params that nothing acts on, so it is worth knowing about
rather than assuming the geometry is complete.

RZ is zero on every model here and carried only so the frame is complete.

the horizontal table, and the wall that rendered twice
------------------------------------------------------

A Helios-32's lasers sit in TWO columns, and the DIFOP says so in a second table that this
driver ignored at first. The measured unit alternates by channel:

    even channels   -3.97 .. -5.32 deg
    odd channels    +3.57 .. +4.80 deg

An 8 degree spread between neighbours. Dropping it does not blur a cloud, it splits one: a
vertical wall renders as two walls swung apart, which is how the omission surfaced -- as
"scattered lines, some way too left and some others way too right" in the viewer.

Worth being precise about why a top-down view is a good test for this. A bird's-eye
projection collapses elevation, and for a VERTICAL surface every channel's projection lands
on the same line no matter its elevation angle, because the horizontal reach r*cos(elev)
grows exactly as the beam's travel along the wall does. So one wall must draw one line. Two
lines means the per-channel azimuth is wrong, and it cannot be an artifact of the projection.

Fitting a line to the even channels and to the odd channels of one 10 degree sector
measures the error directly. On the real unit, over the same points:

    corrections applied   even -36.86 deg, odd -36.93 deg   ->  0.06 deg apart
    corrections zeroed    even -32.23 deg, odd -36.03 deg   ->  3.80 deg apart

The correction is applied in `scan.resolve`, which is why the sin/cos had to move inside the
channel loop in viewer.cpp: hoisting them out is only valid when a firing's channels share
an azimuth, which is exactly what a two-column sensor breaks. The offsets ride in the
calibration frame as `azimuth_offsets_deg`, and a consumer that ignores them gets the split
cloud back.

One residual is known and NOT corrected here: within a block the 32 channels fire
sequentially over 53.63 of the block's 55.56 us, so each channel's true azimuth leads the
block's by up to 0.193 deg at 600 rpm -- rs_driver interpolates this with a per-channel time
fraction. It is a tenth the size of the two-column error and shows as slight smearing rather
than a split; correcting it needs the firing-time table on the wire as well.

Two things the measurements changed beyond constants:

  * a device with no time source still fills the timestamp. This unit reports 2017-01-01,
    so the driver treats anything before 2020 as unset and falls back to the host clock:
    otherwise a consumer measuring latency against it would be seven years out rather than
    obviously unsynchronised.
  * the distance unit is 2.5 mm. This was the last circumstantial figure and it is now
    settled against RoboSense's own driver rather than inferred from the scene: rs_driver's
    decoder_RSHELIOS.hpp initialises DISTANCE_RES to 0.0025f, where decoder_RS16.hpp and
    decoder_RS32.hpp both use 0.005f. The earlier hardware evidence agreed but could not
    prove it -- the largest count in a revolution was 10,834, which is 27 m at 2.5 mm and
    54 m at 5 mm, and both were physically possible for the room.

That same file settles the rest of the layout, and it agrees with every constant the
packet capture had already forced: msop len 1248, a 4-byte MSOP id of 55 AA 05 5A, an
8-byte DIFOP id, block id FF EE, 12 blocks of 32 channels, and a header of id[4] +
protocol_version[2] + reserved[14] before a 10-byte UTC timestamp -- which puts the
timestamp at offset 20 and makes the header 42 bytes, then 4 bytes of index and a 2-byte
tail to reach 1248. Two independent routes to the same numbers is the strongest form this
gets without RoboSense's protocol document.

The drivers now apply the same rated window rs_driver does, rather than passing everything
a count can express: 0.1 to 180 m for the Helios, 0.4 to 230 m for the RS16, from the same
const params as the distance unit. Anything outside becomes 0, the no-return sentinel that
`resolve` already skips, so near-field noise and impossible long returns stop reaching the
cloud. The rule is in one place -- `scan.sampleDistanceMm` -- and each driver passes its own
numbers, because the window differs per model while the logic does not.

Two things about that window are worth knowing:

  * `Sample.distance_mm` is a u32, and was a u16 until the rated window went in. Millimetres
    in a u16 stop at 65.535 m, below both rated maxima, which made the representation rather
    than the sensor the binding limit outdoors -- and the old code clamped anything longer to
    exactly 65535, drawing a point at a range nothing measured. The window is now the only
    thing that rejects a return: the widest a count can express is 65,535 at 5 mm, or 327 m,
    so the field never binds. It cost 2 bytes per sample on the wire, 768 per frame, taking
    a Helios frame from 1613 to 2381 bytes -- 3.6 MB/s at 1500 frames/s, against 2.4 before.
    The Helios' rated 180 m still never binds, since 65,535 counts at 2.5 mm is only 163.8 m;
    the RS16's 230 m does.
  * the widening put a new edge on the write side, where the fakes turn millimetres back
    into counts: a u32 of millimetres can name a range no u16 count can carry. That writes
    0, no return, rather than wrapping -- a sensor that cannot count that high could not have
    reported that range either, and saturating would invent a 163.8 m return. `scan.zig` has
    both halves, `sampleDistanceMm` and `sampleDistanceCounts`, next to each other.

Both were then measured against the unit at 192.168.3.221, reading raw MSOP and histogramming
counts so that a return the driver dropped stays distinguishable from a zero the sensor sent
(downstream they are the same value). Over 1,152,000 samples:

    sensor's own zero   31,862   2.77%
    counts 1..39            67   0.0058%   <- removed by the rated window
    counts >= 40     1,120,071
    max count           10,865             27.16 m

So the window is real but small, and what it removes looks exactly like what it is for: the
sub-minimum counts cluster at 4, 8 and 32..38 -- 10 mm, 20 mm and 80..95 mm -- which is
near-field noise that would otherwise have drawn false points on top of the sensor.

The u32, by contrast, is NOT exercised here. This is an indoor scene: the furthest return in
4.6 million samples was 27.18 m, and nothing came within a factor of two of the 65.535 m
where a u16 used to clamp. The widening is right and the boundary cases are pinned by unit
tests over raw counts, but no run of this example demonstrates it -- it would take a scene
with something 70 m away. Worth knowing before treating a green pipeline as proof.
  * neither fake can exercise the distance window. The nearest return either scene can
    produce is the floor under the steepest downward beam, 1.0 m / sin(49.59 deg) = 1.31 m,
    comfortably inside both windows. So the window is covered by unit tests over raw counts
    and not by running the example -- the same shape of blind spot as the mid-packet azimuth
    wrap the fakes hid, and worth remembering before trusting a green pipeline here.

That asymmetry between the drivers is deliberate. The RS-LiDAR-16's uniform 2 degree
spacing IS its published table, so its default is trustworthy. The Helios has no single
table -- the family covers several distributions -- so any default here would be wrong for
most units.

when nothing is transmitting
----------------------------

A driver whose sensor is absent used to park the thread in recvfrom, where it could not be
interrupted: the last line in the log was "awaiting DIFOP", SIGINT was ignored, and the
process had to be killed. Both drivers now bound both waits.

  receive_timeout_ms       500    one `next` call waits this long, then reports .timeout
  calibration_timeout_ms  3000    how long to wait for the device's angle table

`next` therefore has three outcomes rather than two, and they mean different things:

    .scan       a packet
    .timeout    nothing arrived -- check that the sensor is transmitting, and where to
    .malformed  a datagram arrived and was not ours -- almost always the wrong model,
                since both generations use port 6699 and 1248-byte packets

What that looks like with no sensor running:

    lidar-sensor: no DIFOP within the wait; using the driver's table (32 channels,
      -55.00..19.00 deg) -- these are a placeholder, not this unit's geometry
    lidar-sensor: no packets on 0.0.0.0:6699 for 3s -- is the sensor transmitting, and
      to this host and port?
    ^C
    lidar-sensor: stopping (0 published, 0 malformed, 12 idle waits)

With the wrong driver for the device:

    lidar-sensor: a datagram on 6699 was not helios32 MSOP -- wrong model? the other
      generation uses the same port and size
    lidar-sensor: stopping (0 published, 2994 malformed, 0 idle waits)

And a sensor that appears late is picked up without a restart, including its calibration:

    lidar-sensor: no packets on 0.0.0.0:6699 for 3s -- is the sensor transmitting...
    lidar-sensor: packets are arriving again
    lidar-sensor: 902 packets/s in, ... angles driver
    lidar-sensor: calibration from the device: 600 rpm, channel 0 at -50.00 deg, ...
    lidar-sensor: 1500 packets/s in, ... angles device

The receiver itself is scan.zig's, parameterised by the driver module, because both drivers
had a byte-identical copy of it and this change would have had to be made twice -- which is
how two copies stop being identical. Two tests pin it: a receiver with nobody sending
returns .timeout within its window rather than blocking, and the calibration wait expires
instead of staying open forever without fabricating a device table.

pointing it at real hardware
----------------------------

A sensor PUSHES MSOP to a destination configured on the device; nothing here dials it. So
the sensor's own address is not what the driver is told -- it is only used to discard traffic
from anything else on the port.

    sensor at 192.168.3.221, configured to send to 192.168.3.100
    this host has 192.168.3.100 on en7

    ./zig-out/bin/lidar-sensor helios32 --from 192.168.3.221

    --bind  <addr>   local interface to receive on (default 0.0.0.0, all of them)
    --msop  <port>   MSOP port the sensor sends to (default 6699)
    --difop <port>   DIFOP port (default 7788)
    --from  <addr>   accept only this sensor's packets

If the host does not have the address the sensor is aimed at, nothing arrives and the bind
says so specifically rather than reporting a generic failure. Two sensors aimed at one port
produce one interleaved stream that parses cleanly and describes nothing real, which is what
--from exists to prevent.

running it
----------

    zig build lidar                                  # fake sensor + bridge
    zig build lidar -Dqt=/path/to/Qt/6.10.3/macos    # and the viewer

    ./zig-out/bin/fake-rs16                        # terminal 1: the device
    ./zig-out/bin/lidar-sensor rs16                # terminal 2: driver + publisher
    ./zig-out/bin/lidar-viewer                     # terminal 3: from the project root

or the other sensor, through the same viewer binary:

    ./zig-out/bin/fake-helios32
    ./zig-out/bin/lidar-sensor helios32
    ./zig-out/bin/lidar-viewer '' lidar/helios32

The viewer draws the same revolution two ways. The segmented toggle at the top right swaps
between them, and so does the V key:

    bird           top-down and orthographic, z dropped. Rings every 2 m.
    first person   a pinhole projection from a camera beside the sensor: drag to look,
                   WASD and Q/E to move, wheel or the slider for field of view, R to reset.

    ./zig-out/bin/lidar-viewer '' lidar/helios32 first-person   # open in perspective

Both are worth having for different reasons. The bird view is the measuring instrument: it
drops z, so a vertical surface collapses onto one line and any per-channel azimuth error
shows up as a fan -- which is how the missing horizontal table was caught. The first-person
view is the one that reads as a place rather than a plot, and it is the only one where the
32 channels' vertical spread is visible at all.

The projection is a plain pinhole, and it starts 9 m south of the sensor and 2 m up rather
than at the sensor. That placement is the whole subtlety of the mode, and getting it wrong
is instructive.

A pinhole camera AT the scanner origin is degenerate. Screen radius comes out as
focal * tan(angle between the point and the view axis) -- range cancels exactly -- and a
scan holds exactly one range per direction, so the image is a function of the sampling grid
and of nothing else. It is not a bad picture of the room; it is not a picture of the room.
Every point sits where its channel and azimuth put it whatever it hit, which reads on screen
as curves of evenly spaced points at fixed distances from the centre.

The cheapest way to see this is to flatten the scene: force every range to a constant, which
throws the geometry away entirely, and reproject. Over one revolution of the fake:

    eye at the sensor origin    flattening moves each point     0.00 px
    eye 9 m south, 2 m up       flattening moves each point   383.72 px

Zero, exactly, at the origin -- the image cannot depend on the scene. Range is only visible
through parallax, and parallax needs the eye somewhere the rangefinder is not. Walking back
to the origin still works, and the viewer says so on screen rather than leaving a degenerate
projection to be rediscovered as a bug.

Two smaller details. Points behind the near plane are discarded rather than clamped: a
negative depth would otherwise draw them mirrored in front of the camera. And points off the
viewport edges are culled before the transform, because most of a 360 degree cloud is
off-screen at any one time and Qt would clip them only after transforming every one.

"points shown" now means points actually painted, not the size of the revolution -- in the
bird view with the floor showing those are the same number, and in first person they are
not.

Both fakes take the same --msop and --difop flags as the bridge, deliberately spelled the
same way, because the two sides have to agree and a mismatch is invisible: the bridge just
sits there reporting nothing. If you move one port, move it on both.

    ./zig-out/bin/fake-helios32 127.0.0.1 --msop 6799 --difop 7888
    ./zig-out/bin/lidar-sensor helios32   --msop 6799 --difop 7888

A wrong port is refused rather than defaulted. The fake used to accept a positional MSOP
port only, so `lidar-sensor --difop 7888` against it could never receive a calibration and
fell back to the driver's built-in angle table -- reporting `angles driver`, which looks
like a sensor that declined to send its DIFOP rather than a fake that could not.

What each reports:

    fake-rs16: RS-LiDAR-16 -> 127.0.0.1:6699 (MSOP) and :7788 (DIFOP)
    fake-rs16: 751 packets/s sent, azimuth 4.8 deg

    lidar-sensor: calibration from the device: 600 rpm, channel 0 at -15.00 deg, channel 15 at 15.00 deg
    lidar-sensor: 751 packets/s in, 751 published, 0 malformed, 10 revs, 2376 byte frames, angles device

    lidar-viewer: RS-LiDAR-16, 16 channels, 2 firing(s)/block, angles -15.00..15.00 deg, from the device
    lidar-viewer: 750 frames/s, 288000 points/s, 38 revolutions, 28661 displayed, 0 dropped

and for the Helios, from the same viewer:

    lidar-sensor: calibration from the device: 600 rpm, channel 0 at -50.00 deg, channel 31 at 15.00 deg
    lidar-sensor: 1502 packets/s in, 1502 published, 0 malformed, 10 revs, 2381 byte frames, angles device

    lidar-viewer: RS-Helios-32, 32 channels, 1 firing(s)/block, angles -50.00..15.00 deg, from the device
    lidar-viewer: 1487 frames/s, 571008 points/s, 51 revolutions, 57461 displayed, 0 dropped

`zig build test` includes rs16.zig: a write/parse round trip, refusal of short packets and
bad magic and bad block flags, no-return staying no-return, and a non-uniform DIFOP angle
table surviving the trip. A round trip cannot prove the format matches RoboSense's -- only
hardware settles that -- but it catches the drift that actually happens: an offset changed
on one side, a flipped endianness, a distance unit edited in one place.
